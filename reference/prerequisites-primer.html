Foundational Math, Probability & Matrix Primer

Every probability identity, logarithm/logit rule, calculus trick, and matrix bridge used anywhere in Generalized Linear Models—each demonstrated on 2 to 4 concrete numbers so nothing feels like magic.

Audited Layer 0 Foundation Convention: \(\log(x) \equiv \ln(x)\) (base \(e\)) everywhere

1. Random Variable \(Y_i\), Realization \(y_i\), Support \(\mathcal{Y}\) & Open Mean Space \(\mathcal{M}\)

  • Random variable \(Y_i\) vs. realization \(y_i\): Before data collection, \(Y_i\) is a random data-generating rule; afterward, \(y_i\) is the single observed number on disk. Expectations act on \(Y_i\), never on fixed constants (\(E[y_i] = y_i\)).
  • Support \(\mathcal{Y}\) vs. Mean Space \(\mathcal{M}\): Raw outcomes live in \(\mathcal{Y}\) (e.g., \(\{0,1\}\) or \(\{0,1,2,\dots\}\)), whereas the conditional mean \(\mu_i = E[Y_i \mid x_i]\) lives in a smooth open interval \(\mathcal{M}\) (e.g., \((0,1)\) or \((0,\infty)\)).
    2-number check: Flip a fair coin \(Y \in \{0,1\}\): realized values are only \(0\) or \(1\), yet \(\mu = 0.5 \in (0,1)\) is neither \(0\) nor \(1\)! Non-degenerate GLMs exclude exact boundary means \(\mu_i = 0\) or \(1\) where randomness vanishes and \(\log\frac{\mu_i}{1-\mu_i} = \pm\infty\).
  • Conditional slice \(Y_i \mid x_i\): A GLM does not assume the pooled histogram of \(Y\) is Poisson or Bernoulli; it assigns a separate distribution slice at each covariate value \(x_i\) with mean \(\mu_i = \sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} y\,P(Y_i=y \mid x_i)\) (discrete) or \(\int_{\mathcal{Y}} y\,f(y\mid x_i)\,dy\) (continuous).

2. Indicator Trick (\(0/1\)) & Expectation / Variance Scaling Rules

Why \(E[Y] = P(Y=1)\) & \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\mu(1-\mu)\)

For binary \(Y \in \{0,1\}\) with \(P(Y=1) = \mu\) and \(P(Y=0) = 1-\mu\), squaring leaves \(0\) and \(1\) unchanged (\(Y^2 = Y\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} E[Y] &= 0(1-\mu) + 1(\mu) = \mu, \\ E[Y^2] &= E[Y] = \mu, \\ \operatorname{Var}(Y) &= E[Y^2] - (E[Y])^2 \\ &= \mu - \mu^2 = \mu(1-\mu). \end{aligned} \]

Check at \(\mu = 0.8\): \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 0.8 - 0.64 = 0.16 = 0.8(0.2)\).
Coding trap: If labels are coded \(\{-1, +1\}\), \(E[Y] = 2\mu - 1 \neq \mu\)! GLMs require \(\{0,1\}\) coding.

Linearity & Squared Variance Scaling

For constants \(a, c\):

\[ \begin{aligned} E[aY + c] &= aE[Y] + c, \\ \operatorname{Var}(aY + c) &= a^2\operatorname{Var}(Y). \end{aligned} \]

Count vs. Proportion (\(n=4, \pi=0.5\)): Sum of \(4\) flips \(Y \sim \operatorname{Bin}(4, 0.5)\) has \(E[Y] = 4(0.5) = 2\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 4(0.25) = 1\). Sample proportion \(\tilde{Y} = Y/4\) divides distances by \(4\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(\tilde{Y}) = \frac{1}{4^2}(1) = \frac{0.25}{4} = 0.0625\)!

\(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\)		 \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\) \(\mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R}\)
\(\mathcal{M} = \mathbb{R}\)		 \(E[Y] = \mu\)
\(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \sigma^2\) (decoupled from \(\mu\))
Bernoulli
\(\operatorname{Bern}(\mu)\)		 \(\mu^y (1-\mu)^{1-y}\) \(\mathcal{Y} = \{0,1\}\)
\(\mathcal{M} = (0,1)\)		 \(E[Y] = \mu\)
\(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mu(1-\mu)\)
Binomial
\(Y \sim \operatorname{Bin}(n, \pi)\)		 \(\binom{n}{y}\pi^y (1-\pi)^{n-y}\) Count: \(\{0,\dots,n\}\)
Prop. \(\tilde{y}=y/n\): \(\{0,\frac{1}{n},\dots,1\}\)		 Count: \(\mu = n\pi,\; \operatorname{Var} = n\pi(1-\pi)\)

Poisson
\(\operatorname{Pois}(\mu)\)		 \(\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^y}{y!}\) (\(0!=1\)) \(\mathcal{Y} = \{0,1,2,\dots\}\)
\(\mathcal{M} = (0,\infty)\)		 \(E[Y] = \mu,\; \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mu\).
With exposure \(t_i\): \(\mu_i = \lambda_i t_i\).
Gamma
(Mean \(\mu\), shape \(\nu\))		 \(\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}\!\left(\frac{\nu}{\mu}\right)^\nu y^{\nu-1}e^{-\nu y/\mu}\) \(\mathcal{Y} = (0,\infty)\)
\(\mathcal{M} = (0,\infty)\)		 \(E[Y] = \mu,\; \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{\mu^2}{\nu} = \phi\mu^2\) (\(\phi = 1/\nu\)).
Constant \(\text{CV} = \text{SD}/\mu = \sqrt{\phi}\).

Why Poisson has \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mu\): Split an interval into \(m \to \infty\) tiny independent sub-slots each with rare event chance \(p = \mu/m\). Then \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = mp(1-p) = \mu(1 - \mu/m) \to \mu\). Whenever real-world events arrive in correlated clumps rather than independently, variance exceeds \(\mu\) (overdispersion).

4. Nonlinear Transformations & Jensen’s Inequality (\(E[g(Y)] \neq g(E[Y])\))

Even if every observed count happens to be strictly positive (\(y_i > 0\)), regressing \(\log(Y_i)\) on \(x_i\) estimates \(E[\log Y_i \mid x_i]\), whereas a log-link GLM models \(\log(E[Y_i \mid x_i])\). Because \(\log(\cdot)\) is strictly concave (flattening large numbers more than small ones), Jensen’s inequality guarantees \(E[\log Y] < \log(E[Y])\) whenever \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) > 0\):

2-Number Proof That \(E[\log Y] \neq \log(E[Y])\) (Zero-Free Data!)

Suppose \(Y\) takes values \(1\) and \(9\) with probability \(0.5\) each:

  • GLM target \(\log(E[Y])\): Arithmetic mean is \(E[Y] = 0.5(1) + 0.5(9) = 5 \implies \log(5) \approx \mathbf{1.609}\).
  • OLS-on-\(\log(Y)\) target \(E[\log Y]\): \(0.5\log(1) + 0.5\log(9) = 0 + 0.5(2.197) \approx \mathbf{1.099} = \log(3)\).

Exponentiating \(1.099\) back predicts \(e^{1.099} = \mathbf{3}\) (the geometric mean \(\sqrt{1\times 9}\)) instead of \(5\)—a 40% downward bias! Worse, because count relative variance \(\approx 1/\mu_i(x_i)\) changes with \(x_i\), this Jensen gap varies across \(x_i\) and biases the slope \(\hat{\beta}_1\), not just the intercept. And if zeros occur, the hack \(\log(y_i + \epsilon)\) makes \(\hat{\beta}_1\) arbitrarily sensitive to whether \(\epsilon = 0.1\) (\(\log\epsilon = -2.3\)) or \(10^{-6}\) (\(\log\epsilon = -13.8\))!

5. Log / Exp Laws, Probability \(\leftrightarrow\) Odds \(\leftrightarrow\) Logit & Sigmoid Inversion

Universal logarithm convention: In statistics, R, and Python (`np.log`), \(\log(x)\) always denotes the natural logarithm base \(e\) (\(\ln x\)), satisfying \(\exp(\log u) = u\), \(\log(ab) = \log a + \log b\), and \(\log(a^y) = y\log a\) (never confuse \(\log(1+e^\theta)\) with \(\log 1 + \theta\)!).

3-Line Sigmoid & \(1-\pi\) Inversion

Given \(\theta = \log\!\left(\frac{\pi}{1-\pi}\right)\), exponentiate both sides:

\[ \begin{aligned} e^\theta = \frac{\pi}{1-\pi} &\implies e^\theta(1-\pi) = \pi \\ &\implies \pi(1+e^\theta) = e^\theta, \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \pi &= \frac{e^\theta}{1+e^\theta} = \frac{1}{1+e^{-\theta}}, \\ 1 - \pi &= \frac{1}{1+e^\theta}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore \(\log(1-\pi) = -\log(1+e^\theta)\)—the exact step that produces Bernoulli’s cumulant \(b(\theta) = \log(1+e^\theta)\)!

6. Six Calculus & Likelihood Building Blocks

  1. Differentiating key cumulants \(b(\theta)\) twice:
    • Bernoulli \(b(\theta) = \log(1+e^\theta)\): \(b'(\theta) = \frac{e^\theta}{1+e^\theta} = 1 - (1+e^\theta)^{-1} = \mu\); chain rule gives \(b''(\theta) = (1+e^\theta)^{-2}e^\theta = \frac{e^\theta}{1+e^\theta}\frac{1}{1+e^\theta} = \mu(1-\mu)\).
    • Gamma \(b(\theta) = -\log(-\theta)\) for \(\theta < 0\) (note \(-\theta > 0\)!): \(b'(\theta) = -\frac{1}{-\theta}(-1) = -\frac{1}{\theta} = \mu\); \(b''(\theta) = \frac{1}{\theta^2} = \mu^2\).
  2. Inverse-function derivative rule (\(\frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{1}{dy/dx}\)): Do not confuses functional inverse \(g^{-1}(\eta)\) with reciprocal \(1/g(\mu)\)! For derivatives, slopes reciprocate: since \(\mu = b'(\theta)\), \(\frac{d\mu}{d\theta} = b''(\theta) = V(\mu) \implies \frac{d\theta}{d\mu} = \frac{1}{V(\mu)}\); since \(\eta = g(\mu)\), \(\frac{d\mu}{d\eta} = \frac{1}{g'(\mu)}\).
  3. Log-derivative trick: Since \(f(y\mid\theta) = e^{\log f(y\mid\theta)}\), chain rule gives \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta} = \frac{\partial \log f}{\partial\theta}\,f(y\mid\theta)\), turning density derivatives directly into expectations!
  4. Swapping \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\) and \(\sum / \int\) (Fixed-Support Condition): Because \(\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}} f(y\mid\theta) = \frac{1}{1+e^\theta} + \frac{e^\theta}{1+e^\theta} = 1\) for all \(\theta\), differentiating wrt \(\theta\) gives \(-\frac{e^\theta}{(1+e^\theta)^2} + \frac{e^\theta}{(1+e^\theta)^2} = 0\). Interchanging \(\frac{d}{d\theta}\) and \(\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\) requires the support \(\mathcal{Y}\) not to depend on \(\theta\) (counterexample: \(Y \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0,\theta)\) has moving upper limit \(\theta\), so \(\int_0^\theta \partial_\theta(1/\theta)\,dy = -1/\theta \neq 0\); exponential families are safe because \(\mathcal{Y}\) is free of \(\theta\)).
  5. First-order Taylor linearization (for IRLS working response \(z_i\)): Expanding \(g(y_i)\) around \(\hat{\mu}_i\) gives \(z_i = g(\hat{\mu}_i) + (y_i - \hat{\mu}_i)g'(\hat{\mu}_i) = \hat{\eta}_i + (y_i - \hat{\mu}_i)\frac{d\eta_i}{d\mu_i}\). At \(y_1 = 0, \hat{\mu}_1 = 1\) under \(g(\mu)=\log\mu\), raw \(\log(0) = -\infty\) crashes, while \(z_1 = \log(1) + \frac{0-1}{1} = -1\) is completely finite!
  6. Delta-method variance (for IRLS weights \(w_i\)): Treating trial \(\hat{\eta}_i\) and slope \(g'(\hat{\mu}_i)\) as fixed constants at step \(t\), \(\operatorname{Var}(z_i) = [g'(\hat{\mu}_i)]^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = \frac{\phi}{p_i}V(\hat{\mu}_i)[g'(\hat{\mu}_i)]^2\).

7. Design Matrices, Summation-to-Matrix Rules, WLS & \(\chi^2 / F\) Limits

  • Design matrix \(X\) & \(p = \dim(\beta)\) convention: With \(n\) rows and \(p\) total parameters (including intercept column \(\mathbf{1}_n\)), row \(i\) is \(x_i^\top = [1, x_{i1}, \dots, x_{i,p-1}]\), so \(\eta = X\beta\) and residual degrees of freedom are \(\text{df}_{\text{resid}} = n - p\). A categorical factor with \(K\) levels uses \(K-1\) 0/1 dummy columns (dropping one reference baseline so columns don’t sum to \(\mathbf{1}_n\) and make \(X^\top W X\) singular).
  • Three summation-to-matrix identities: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - \mu_i) = X^\top(y - \mu), \qquad \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i x_i^\top = X^\top W X, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(AY) = A\operatorname{Cov}(Y)A^\top. \]
  • Weighted Least Squares (WLS) in 3 numbers: When \(z_i = x_i^\top\beta + e_i\) has unequal variance \(\operatorname{Var}(e_i) = \phi / w_i\), weighting each squared residual by precision \(w_i\) yields \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{WLS}} = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W z\) with \(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_{\text{WLS}}) = \phi(X^\top W X)^{-1}\).
    Check on \(x=(0,1,2)^\top, z=(1,2,6)^\top\): Equal weights give slope \(2.5\); downweighting noisy row 3 with \(w=(4,4,1)\) gives \(X^\top W X = \begin{bmatrix}9&6\\6&8\end{bmatrix}, X^\top W z = \begin{bmatrix}18\\20\end{bmatrix} \implies \hat{\beta}_{\text{WLS}} = (2/3,\; 2.0)^\top\)!
  • \(\chi^2_\nu\) vs. \(F_{\nu, n-p}\) when testing \(\nu = p_2 - p_1\) parameters: When dispersion \(\phi = 1\) is known (Binomial/Poisson), test statistics follow \(\chi^2_\nu\) asymptotically (95% cutoff for \(\nu=1\) is \(3.84 = 1.96^2\)). When \(\hat{\phi}\) is estimated on \(n - p_2\) df (Gaussian/Gamma/Quasi-GLM), dividing by \(\hat{\phi}\) gives \(F_{\nu, n-p_2}\); as \(n - p_2 \to \infty\), \(\nu F_{\nu, n-p_2} \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_\nu\).
