Lesson 0002 · Evaluation Metrics Track (Zero Statistics Assumed)

Why We Choose AUC Over Accuracy

No formulas to memorize and zero prior statistics needed: start from the single most intuitive picture of binary classification—pushing true \(0\)s near \(0\) and true \(1\)s near \(1\)—then see why Accuracy misreads that picture and what AUC actually counts.

~10 min interactive lesson Summary Sheet: Metrics Cheat Sheet

1. Start With Direct Intuition: What Model Capability Looks Like on \([0, 1]\)

Before touching any evaluation metric, picture what binary classification is actually doing. Every real-world case belongs to one of two true classes: Class \(0\) (, say a healthy patient or normal card swipe) or Class \(1\) (×, say a sick patient or stolen card).

When you feed those cases into a binary GLM (like Logistic Regression from Lesson 1), the model does not output a blunt "Yes" or "No." Instead, it places every single person along a horizontal scale from \(0.0\) to \(1.0\)—their predicted probability or risk score \(s_i\).

The Most Intuitive Rule of Binary Classification

Generally speaking, the stronger a model’s capability is, the more cleanly the two classes stay near opposite ends of \([0, 1]\): true \(0\)s () should stay near \(0.0\), and true \(1\)s (×) should stay near \(1.0\)!

Interactive Dot Plot: Drag Model Capability & Watch \(0\)s and \(1\)s Pull Apart

  • Weak / blind model (\(0\%\)): Cannot tell the two groups apart, so and × huddle together in the middle (\(\approx 0.50\)).
  • Realistic good model (\(60\%\), default view): Pushes most true \(0\)s () left toward \(0.0\) and most true \(1\)s (×) right toward \(1.0\), while genuinely hard borderline cases still stack in the same middle columns (\(\approx 0.32\text{–}0.66\)).
  • Strong capability (\(100\%\)): Packs true \(0\)s tightly near \(0.0\) and true \(1\)s tightly near \(1.0\), shrinking the middle overlap to zero!

Why do we say "generally speaking"? Two quick reasons: (1) in real life, two patients with identical symptoms can still have different outcomes, leaving unavoidable middle overlap; and (2) as you are about to see in Trap 2, a poorly scaled model can still sort left of × correctly even when its raw numbers get squashed away from \(1.0\) (we connect rank order vs. probability scale in Lesson 0003!).

2. Two Everyday Traps Where Accuracy Misreads That Picture

Now look at how Accuracy tries to judge that \([0, 1]\) dot plot: it drops one fixed vertical cutoff knife \(t\) (software almost always defaults to \(t = 0.50\)), labels everything to the left "\(0\)" and everything to the right "\(1\)," and asks: "Out of everyone, what percentage of Yes/No guesses matched?" That sounds completely fair—until you watch it fail in two opposite ways on tiny numbers Fawcett, 2006, §1–§2:

Trap 1: The "Lazy Brick" (\(99\%\) Accuracy, Zero Value)

Imagine \(100\) card swipes: \(99\) are honest (\(0\)) and \(1\) is a thief (\(1\)).

Suppose we replace our model with a painted wooden brick that prints "Normal (\(0\))" for every customer forever:

  • Honest customers guessed right: \(99 / 99\)
  • Thieves caught: \(0 / 1\) (missed the thief!)
  • Total Accuracy: \(99 / 100 = 99\%\)!

Whenever \(1\)s are rare (fraud, disease, server crashes, ad clicks), Accuracy mostly congratulates you for saying "nothing happened."

Trap 2: The Cutoff Illusion (\(60\%\) vs. \(100\%\) on the Same Model)

Look at our GLM’s risk scores on 5 clinic patients (\(0=\) Healthy, \(1=\) Sick):

Patient Truth \(y_i\) GLM Score \(s_i\) Guess at \(t=0.50\)
P100.080 ✓
P200.180 ✓
P300.320 ✓
P410.420 ✗
P510.480 ✗

Look at column 3: every sick patient (\(0.42, 0.48\)) got a higher score than every healthy patient (\(0.08, 0.18, 0.32\))! Yet software defaults to cutoff \(t = 0.50\) ("predict \(1\) only if \(s_i \ge 0.50\)"), giving \(3/5 = 60\%\) Accuracy! Lower the cutoff to \(0.40\), and Accuracy jumps to \(5/5 = 100\%\)—without changing the model!

Why Accuracy Confuses Two Separate Jobs

Accuracy does not measure your model alone; it measures your model glued to one arbitrary cutoff \(t\), weighted heavily by whichever group (\(0\) or \(1\)) happens to be bigger. To judge the model fairly, we must separate the two real groups and look across cutoffs!

3. What ROC and AUC Actually Mean (Zero Calculus Needed!)

First, instead of mixing Healthy (\(0\)) and Sick (\(1\)) people into one pot, split them into their two real groups at any alarm cutoff \(t\):

  • Catch Rate (officially True Positive Rate, TPR, or Recall): Among truly Sick people (\(1\)s), what fraction did our alarm catch?
  • False Alarm Rate (officially False Positive Rate, FPR): Among truly Healthy people (\(0\)s), what fraction did we wrongly scare with a false alarm?

As you slide cutoff \(t\) from super strict (\(1.00\), alarm never rings \(\to (0,0)\)) down to super loose (\(0.00\), alarm rings for everyone \(\to (1,1)\)), plotting \((\text{False Alarm Rate}, \text{Catch Rate})\) traces the ROC Curve (Receiver Operating Characteristic—a World War II radar name for separating real planes from static noise!).

The Secret Shortcut: AUC Is Just a Pairwise Contest!

"Area Under the ROC Curve" (AUC) sounds like geometry, but statisticians proved it equals something anyone can count on their fingers Hanley & McNeil, 1982, p. 30 Fawcett, 2006, §7.2:

The One-Sentence Meaning of AUC

Pick one real \(1\) (Sick) and one real \(0\) (Healthy) at random. AUC is the fraction of \((1, 0)\) pairs where your model gave a higher score to the real \(1\) than to the real \(0\)! (Exact score ties count as half a win: \(0.5\).)

Let’s count AUC by hand on 5 patients where scores overlap slightly: 3 Healthy people get 0.10 (Anna), 0.30 (Ben), 0.45 (Dan), and 2 Sick people get 0.40 (Cara), 0.80 (Eva). With \(2\) Sick and \(3\) Healthy people, there are only \(2 \times 3 = 6\) possible pairs:

  • Eva (Sick, \(0.80\)) vs. Anna (\(0.10\)) ✓, vs. Ben (\(0.30\)) ✓, vs. Dan (\(0.45\)) ✓ \(\longrightarrow\) \(3\) wins!
  • Cara (Sick, \(0.40\)) vs. Anna (\(0.10\)) ✓, vs. Ben (\(0.30\)) ✓, vs. Dan (\(0.45\)) ✗ \(\longrightarrow\) \(2\) wins, \(1\) loss!

Total score: \(5\) wins out of \(6\) pairs \(\implies \text{AUC} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833\)! Notice why AUC immediately fixes both Accuracy traps:

  1. No arbitrary cutoff \(t\): We never needed \(0.50\)—we compared the scores directly!
  2. Exposes the Lazy Brick instantly: If a lazy fraud model gives the same score (say \(0.10\)) to all \(99\) honest shoppers and \(1\) thief, every single pair is a tie (\(0.5\) points) \(\implies \mathbf{\text{AUC} = 0.50}\), the exact score of flipping a blind coin!

5. Two Honest Blind Spots: When Even ROC-AUC Needs Backup

A great practitioner knows both why AUC beats Accuracy and the two situations where ROC-AUC alone is not enough (full table in Metrics Cheat Sheet §3):

  1. Extreme Needle-in-a-Haystack (\(1\) thief in \(10{,}000\) shoppers) \(\longrightarrow\) Check PR-AUC too: False Alarm Rate divides false alarms by all honest shoppers. If your alarm flags \(1\) real thief plus \(100\) innocent shoppers, \(\text{FPR} = 100 / 10{,}000 = 1\%\) looks tiny and ROC-AUC looks near \(0.99\)—even though \(100\) out of \(101\) alarms (\(99\%\)) are false! When false alarms waste human investigators’ time, check Precision and PR-AUC Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015.
  2. When Exact Probabilities Matter \(\longrightarrow\) Check Log-Loss (GLM Deviance): As you saw with the Squash Scores ÷ 2 button, AUC only checks rank order. Dividing every probability by \(2\) leaves AUC unchanged, even though telling a patient their \(80\%\) surgical risk is "\(40\%\)" is dangerous! That is why Logistic GLMs fit parameters using Deviance (= \(2 \times\) Log-Loss), which rewards honest, calibrated probabilities Gneiting & Raftery, 2007.
