How Metrics Map to the Four Categories of Model Performance

"Is this model performing well?" is not one question—it is four completely separate questions. Once you see the four categories of model performance, you will never confuse Accuracy, ROC-AUC, Calibration, and GLM Deviance again.

~10 min interactive lesson Builds on: Lesson 0002 (AUC vs. Accuracy) Cheat Sheet: Metrics Guide

1. Why "Model Performance" Splits Into Four Distinct Categories

Recall our core visual intuition from Lesson 0002: generally speaking, a stronger binary model pushes true \(0\)s () near \(0.0\) and true \(1\)s (×) near \(1.0\). Why, then, do statisticians still distinguish four categories of metrics? Because checking whether a model truly achieved that \([0, 1]\) picture splits into four distinct questions Steyerberg et al., 2010, and each metric answers only one:

Category 1 · Discrimination (Ranking / Separation)

Plain question: "Do real \(1\)s get higher scores than real \(0\)s?"
Metrics: ROC-AUC, PR-AUC.
Blind to: Whether the numbers are honest probabilities, and any single cutoff \(t\).

Category 2 · Calibration (Probability Honesty)

Plain question: "When the model says \(0.75\), does the event actually happen ~\(75\%\) of the time?"
Metrics: Calibration Curve (45° plot), ECE (average bucket gap).
Blind to: Whether scores actually separate individuals!

Category 3 · Overall Probabilistic Performance

Plain question: "How close is each probability \(s_i\) to the true \(0/1\) outcome overall?"
Metrics: Log-Loss (\(= \frac{\text{GLM Deviance}}{2n}\)), Brier Score \(\frac{1}{n}\sum(y_i-s_i)^2\) Murphy, 1973.
Key rule: Only scores well when both Category 1 AND Category 2 succeed!

Category 4 · Cutoff-Specific Action / Utility

Plain question: "Once we lock in ONE cutoff \(t\) to trigger Yes/No actions, how many mistakes happen?"
Metrics: Accuracy, Recall (TPR), FPR, Precision, F1.
Key rule: Evaluates Model + Cutoff \(t\) together, never the model alone!

Why Accuracy vs. AUC Was Really a Category Mistake

In Lesson 0002, Accuracy failed because people grabbed a Category 4 metric (tied to default cutoff \(t=0.50\)) when they actually wanted to measure Category 1: Can the model separate \(1\)s from \(0\)s? (ROC-AUC).

2. The 2×2 Diagnosis Grid & How AUC Grades Discrimination

Beginners often assume: "If \(\text{AUC} = 0.94\), my probabilities must be accurate!"—or its opposite: "If my probabilities match the true average, my model must be smart!" Both are false! Suppose \(4\) of \(8\) clinic patients are sick (\(50\%\) overall):

Model Archetype Cat 1: Discrimination Cat 2: Calibration Real-World Verdict
A. Sharp & Honest GLM
Buckets avg \(0.25\) & \(0.75\); sick rates \(1/4\) & \(3/4\)		 High (\(\text{AUC}=0.938\)) Honest (\(\text{ECE}=0.000\)) Gold standard: separates sick from healthy and gives trustworthy risk numbers for clinical/pricing math.
B. Squashed Ranker (\(\div 5\))
Predicts \(0.05\) & \(0.15\) instead of \(0.25\) & \(0.75\)		 High (\(\text{AUC}=0.938\)) Broken (\(\text{ECE}=0.400\)) Good sorter, dangerous liar: fine for sorting a top-10 queue, disastrous if a doctor trusts "\(15\%\) risk" when \(75\%\) are sick!
C. Constant \(0.50\) Shrug
Predicts \(0.50\) for all 8 patients		 Zero (\(\text{AUC}=0.500\)) Honest (\(\text{ECE}=0.000\)) Honest but useless: saying "\(50\%\)" when half are sick is 100% calibrated, yet separates nobody!
D. Overconfident (\(0.01 / 0.99\))
Exaggerates \(25\%\to 1\%\) and \(75\%\to 99\%\)		 High (\(\text{AUC}=0.938\)) Extreme (\(\text{ECE}=0.238\)) Hidden Log-Loss bomb: AUC and Accuracy@\(0.50\) look great, but one wrong \(99\%\) bet ruins financial/medical decisions!

How Numeric AUC Values Classify Discrimination Strength

Within Category 1 (Discrimination), applied logistic regression uses a standard rough scale Hosmer, Lemeshow & Sturdivant, 2013, p. 177—always paired with domain common sense:

  • \(\text{AUC} = 0.50\): No discrimination (blind coin flip or constant score; \(<0.50\) means predictions point backward!).
  • \(0.50 < \text{AUC} < 0.70\): Weak separation (heavy overlap—though in noisy fields like finance or ad clicks, \(0.60\) can still earn millions).
  • \(0.70 \le \text{AUC} < 0.80\): Acceptable / useful separation (typical solid clinical or credit-risk GLM).
  • \(0.80 \le \text{AUC} < 0.90\): Strong separation; \(\ge 0.90\): Outstanding separation (Tip: if \(\text{AUC} > 0.99\) on messy human data, check first whether the answer leaked into your input columns!).

4. How All Four Categories Connect to Generalized Linear Models

Now the entire GLM workflow clicks into one logical pipeline:

  1. During Fitting (Category 3 \(\to\) Category 2): Logistic Regression estimates \(\hat{\beta}\) by minimizing Residual Deviance \(D = 2n \times \text{Log-Loss}\) (EDF & Deviance Reference). Because Log-Loss is a strictly proper scoring rule Gneiting & Raftery, 2007, fitting a well-specified GLM automatically pushes predicted means \(\hat{\mu}_i\) toward honest Calibration (for instance, with an intercept, average predicted probability equals the exact sample event rate!).
  2. During Model Comparison (Category 1 + Category 2): Report ROC-AUC (or PR-AUC for rare events) to measure separation power, plus a Calibration Curve / Log-Loss check to confirm the probabilities themselves remain trustworthy.
  3. At Business Deployment (Category 4): Only when an action is required (block a card, order a biopsy) do you pick a cutoff \(t\) based on the real-world cost of a False Alarm vs. a Missed Case—and report Recall, Precision, or Net Benefit at that chosen \(t\).
