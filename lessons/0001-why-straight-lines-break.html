Lesson 0001 · Foundations of Generalized Linear Models

Why Straight Lines Break—and the Three-Part GLM Fix

Before touching exponential-family algebra, let’s watch Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) fail on two 4-row datasets—and see the exact surgical change that invents Generalized Linear Models.

~10 min interactive lesson Foundation: Prerequisites Primer

1. Two Tiny Datasets Where Ordinary Linear Regression Breaks

Ordinary linear regression models each observation as a straight line plus constant-variance Gaussian noise Rodríguez, App. B §B.1:

\[ Y_i = \mu_i + \epsilon_i, \qquad \mu_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i, \qquad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2). \]

Let’s fit that exact OLS straight line to two tiny 4-observation datasets with small numbers. Look closely at the rightmost column in each table:

Notice that two separate assumptions fail at the same time:

  1. Broken Mean Range (Systematic mismatch): Any non-flat line \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\) eventually crosses below \(0\) and above \(1\), whereas probabilities live in \((0,1)\) and expected counts live in \((0, +\infty)\).
  2. Broken Constant-Variance Rule (Stochastic mismatch): OLS assumes constant variance \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i \mid x_i) = \sigma^2\) everywhere (homoscedasticity). In reality, non-Normal data have built-in heteroscedasticity:
    • For binary outcomes \(Y_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(\mu_i)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i \mid x_i) = \mu_i(1-\mu_i)\). At \(\mu_i = 0.50\), variance is \(0.25\); at \(\mu_i = 0.99\), variance drops \(25\times\) to \(0.0099\) because outcomes are almost always \(1\).
    • For Poisson counts \(Y_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\mu_i)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i \mid x_i) = \mu_i\). Busy hours with mean \(25\) vary much more (\(\text{SD} = 5\)) than quiet hours with mean \(1\) (\(\text{SD} = 1\)).

2. The Classic Trap: Why Not Just Transform the Data \(y_i\)?

A natural first instinct on Dataset B is: “Counts must be positive—why don’t we just take \(\log(y_i)\) and run ordinary linear regression?”

Try it on Row 4 of Dataset B (\(x_4 = 5,\; y_4 = 0\)):

\[ \log(y_4) = \log(0) = -\infty \quad\text{(crashes immediately!)} \]

On Dataset A (\(y_i \in \{0, 1\}\)), trying to apply the logit transform \(\log\!\left(\frac{y_i}{1 - y_i}\right)\) (where \(\log \equiv \ln\), natural log base \(e\)) to raw outcomes gives \(\log(0) = -\infty\) when \(y_i=0\) and \(\log(1/0) = +\infty\) when \(y_i=1\)—every single row blows up! And even when counts are strictly positive—say \(Y \in \{1, 9\}\) with probability \(0.5\) each—regressing \(\log(Y)\) models \(E[\log Y] = 0.5\log 1 + 0.5\log 9 = \log 3 \approx 1.10\) (exponentiating back to \(3\)), which by Jensen’s inequality underestimates \(\log(E[Y]) = \log 5 \approx 1.61\) Rodríguez, App. B p. 3 (Prerequisites §4).

The Core GLM Insight (Nelder & Wedderburn, 1972)

Do not transform the raw response \(Y_i\); transform its conditional mean \(\mu_i = E[Y_i \mid x_i]\). In Dataset B at \(x_4 = 5\), the observed count is \(y_4 = 0\), which is a completely valid draw from a Poisson distribution with linear predictor \(\eta_4 = 1.80 - 0.65(5) = -1.45\) and expected count \(\mu_4 = e^{-1.45} \approx 0.235 > 0\)—so \(\log(\mu_4) = -1.45\) is finite and well-behaved!

3. The Three-Part GLM Blueprint & Interactive Explorer

John Nelder and Robert Wedderburn unified linear, logistic, and Poisson regression into a single architecture with three modular parts Nelder & Wedderburn, 1972 McCullagh & Nelder, 1989, Ch. 2 (summarized on your one-page GLM Blueprint Sheet):

  1. Random Component: Choose a distribution for \(Y_i \mid x_i\) (Bernoulli, Poisson, Normal, Gamma, …) that matches the outcome type and fixes how \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i \mid x_i)\) depends on \(\mu_i\).
  2. Systematic Component (Linear Predictor): Combine covariates linearly as \(\eta_i = x_i^\top \beta\) on the unrestricted real line \((-\infty, +\infty)\).
  3. Link Function: Choose a smooth, invertible bridge \(g(\mu_i) = \eta_i\)—equivalently \(\mu_i = g^{-1}(\eta_i)\)—mapping the restricted domain of \(\mu_i\) onto \((-\infty, +\infty)\).
Scale Translation: Additive on \(\eta_i\) \(\Longleftrightarrow\) Multiplicative on \(\mu_i\) (or Odds)

Under a log link \(\log(\mu_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\), increasing \(x_i\) by \(+1\) adds \(\beta_1\) to \(\log(\mu_i)\), which multiplies the expected count \(\mu_i = e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i}\) by \(e^{\beta_1}\) (since \(e^{\eta_i + \beta_1} = e^{\eta_i}\cdot e^{\beta_1}\)). Under a logit link, \(+1\) in \(x_i\) adds \(\beta_1\) to log-odds and multiplies the odds \(\frac{\mu_i}{1-\mu_i}\) by \(e^{\beta_1}\).

Interactive Lab: Watch \(\eta \to \mu \to \operatorname{Var}(Y \mid x)\) vs. OLS

Try this (30 seconds): (1) On Dataset A, drag \(x^*\) from \(4.0\) to \(8.5\): watch OLS cross into the pink forbidden zone (\(>1\)), while the teal GLM curve flattens smoothly below \(1\) and its shaded \(\pm 1\text{ SD}\) band (\(\sqrt{\mu(1-\mu)}\); note raw binary draws still jump to \(0\) or \(1\)) pinches narrower! (2) Switch to Dataset B at \(x^* = 5.0\): see how OLS predicts negative customers (\(-0.58\)) while the Poisson GLM predicts \(\mu \approx 0.235 > 0\) with small spread.
